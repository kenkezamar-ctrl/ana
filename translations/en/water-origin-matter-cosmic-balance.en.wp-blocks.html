A study of formation, growth, and stability, with an exploratory hypothesis concerning a fifth force.

Khenchela – Algeria

Preliminary version, open to further development and scientific documentation.

Abstract

This study examines water from physical, chemical, and cosmological perspectives and discusses an exploratory hypothesis proposing the possible existence of a primordial constituent or an undiscovered physical field that could influence the formation, growth, and stability of celestial bodies. The proposal begins from the possibility that matter and the cosmic environment may influence one another, and that an uneven distribution of a hypothetical constituent could affect the stability of certain systems. The study distinguishes established scientific knowledge from open questions and hypotheses that still require observational and experimental evidence. It does not assume in advance that water is the source of dark matter or dark energy; such a claim is treated as unproven and requiring an independent test.

Keywords

Water; origin of hydrogen; cosmic formation; stability of celestial bodies; fifth force; dark matter; dark energy; scientific method.

Introduction

Water is one of the most important known chemical compounds because of its central role in biological, geological, and climatic processes. A water molecule consists of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, and its chemical formula is H₂O. Water molecules are also found in various cosmic environments, including molecular clouds, circumstellar disks, and some icy bodies (van Dishoeck et al., 2013).

This study begins with a broader question: could unknown physical interactions or components contribute to certain aspects of the formation and stability of celestial bodies? The author proposes examining the possibility of a constituent or field that acts on matter and the cosmic environment, such that its distribution and interactions might, hypothetically, be factors in formation, growth, and stability. Scientific methodology requires that this proposal not be treated as fact until it has been defined mathematically and has yielded testable predictions.

Research Problem

To what extent does the physical and chemical understanding of water and the origin of its elements explain its role in cosmic systems, and what conditions would be required to turn the hypothesis of an additional constituent influencing the formation, growth, and stability of celestial bodies into a testable scientific model?

Research Objectives

  • Present the fundamental properties of water in precise scientific language.
  • Explain the cosmic origin of hydrogen and the formation of oxygen in stars.
  • Distinguish established mechanisms governing the formation and stability of stars and planets.
  • Formulate the primordial-constituent and cosmic-balance hypothesis in a way that allows critical examination.
  • Identify data, experiments, and observations that could test the hypothesis.

Chapter I: Physical and Chemical Properties of Water

Water is a polar compound. Hydrogen bonds between its molecules influence several of its properties, including its relatively high specific heat capacity, high heat of vaporization, surface tension, and the variation of its density with temperature. At ordinary atmospheric pressure, pure water reaches a maximum density of approximately 1 g/cm³ at about 4 °C. Water expands upon freezing; consequently, under ordinary conditions, ice is less dense than liquid water.

The properties of a water sample are affected by its content of salts, gases, and dissolved substances, as well as by temperature, pressure, and contaminants. These factors can be measured through physical and chemical analysis. They should not be confused with the claim that water retains a special memory of information or words: reliable scientific evidence does not establish such an effect in the commonly asserted sense.

Equation for the Formation of Water

Water can be formed from hydrogen and oxygen according to the balanced chemical equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. This is a known reaction that releases energy under suitable conditions. Its practical implementation, however, requires strict safety controls because a hydrogen–oxygen mixture can ignite and explode.

Chapter II: The Origin of Hydrogen, Oxygen, and Water in the Universe

Most hydrogen formed during the early stages of the universe's history, after the Big Bang. Oxygen formed later through stellar nucleosynthesis and was then dispersed into the interstellar medium by stellar winds and stellar explosions. In cold environments, water molecules can form through chemical reactions on the surfaces of dust grains or in the gas; depending on conditions, they may subsequently exist as vapor or ice (Cazaux et al., 2010; van Dishoeck et al., 2013).

One ion relevant to the history of cosmic chemistry is helium hydride, HeH⁺, which has been detected astronomically in the planetary nebula NGC 7027. Its significance is discussed in the context of early molecular chemistry, but this observation does not amount to a direct observation of the molecule in the primordial universe (Güsten et al., 2019).

The presence of water in the universe does not mean that stars themselves are generally made of stable water. Stars consist primarily of plasma under extreme temperatures and pressures. A distinction must therefore be made between the presence of water in suitable cosmic environments and the claim that water is the origin of all forms of matter.

Chapter III: Cosmic Formation, Growth, and Stability

First: Formation

Stars form through the gravitational collapse of dense regions within clouds of gas and dust. Planets form from material in disks surrounding young stars through collisions, accretion, and dynamical and chemical interactions.

Second: Growth

A celestial body grows when it accretes matter or when its internal structure changes through physical processes. Growth varies according to the body's mass, composition, environment, radiation level, and surrounding gravitational interactions.

Third: Stability and Collapse

A star's stability depends on internal balances that change as it evolves, while orbits are determined by gravity and the motion of celestial bodies. Collapse may occur when pressure or resistance mechanisms can no longer balance gravity. These mechanisms are known; any hypothesis proposing an additional factor must therefore provide an effect that can be distinguished from existing explanations.

Chapter IV: The Primordial-Constituent and Fifth-Force Hypothesis

The hypothesis under consideration proposes the possibility of an undiscovered primordial constituent, or an additional physical field, that acts on matter and the cosmic environment. Under this proposal, its effects might depend on its quantity, distribution, and interaction strength with each celestial body, potentially affecting formation, growth, and stability. An increase or decrease is considered as a possible source of imbalance, not as an established cause of collapse.

The term “fifth force” is commonly used for a hypothetical interaction beyond the four known fundamental interactions: gravity, electromagnetism, the strong interaction, and the weak interaction. Naming such a force does not establish its existence. Its mediator or field, coupling to matter, range, strength, and experimental distinguishability from known forces must be specified.

Formulation of the Hypothesis

The study assumes that an additional constituent or field, if demonstrated to exist, might alter certain balance conditions in matter and cosmic systems. This possibility should be tested by defining its physical properties, formulating an interaction law, deriving quantitative predictions, and comparing them with measurements. The hypothesis remains exploratory until supported by independent evidence.

Chapter V: Dark Matter and Dark Energy

Dark matter refers to a component whose existence is inferred primarily from its gravitational effects. Dark energy is the term used for the cause associated with the accelerated expansion of the universe in the standard cosmological model. These are distinct concepts, and no reliable evidence establishes that either consists of water or of the constituent proposed in this study (Planck Collaboration, 2020; Particle Data Group, 2024).

The study may ask whether the hypothetical model produces a new observational effect, but it must not equate a question with evidence. Any proposed connection requires a mathematically consistent explanation in relation to data on galaxies, gravitational lensing, the cosmic microwave background, and the expansion of the universe.

Chapter VI: Preliminary Mathematical Model and Test Conditions

To organize the proposal, let ρₓ denote the density of the hypothetical constituent and ρ_eq a hypothetical reference value for equilibrium. The deviation is then defined by: Δρₓ = ρₓ − ρ_eq. This relation is a mathematical definition of deviation; it is not a physical law proving the hypothesis.

A general relation for a stability indicator S may also be written as dS/dt = f(ρₓ, M, T, P, …), where M is the body's mass, T its temperature, and P its pressure. The function f cannot be determined, nor collapse inferred from it, before the nature of the constituent, its interaction mechanism, measurement units, and initial and boundary conditions have been specified.

Additional notation clarification: S denotes a hypothetical stability indicator here, not entropy, to avoid confusion with its common use in thermodynamics. The measurement method and unit of this indicator must be defined; accordingly, the unit of the right-hand side f must be the unit of S divided by time. ρₓ and ρ_eq must have the same unit, and the physical basis for the reference value ρ_eq must be defined. The relation dS/dt = f(ρₓ, M, T, P, …) remains schematic until the function f, independent variables, physical mechanism, and initial and boundary conditions are specified.

Any serious model must respect conservation of energy and momentum, explain why its effects have not previously been detected, and produce quantitative, falsifiable predictions rather than a general description that can be reinterpreted after any outcome.

Chapter VII: Proposed Methodology

  • Review peer-reviewed scientific literature on water in space, element formation, and the evolution of stars and planets.
  • Define the hypothetical constituent: is it a particle, a field, or a form of interaction?
  • Identify its measurable properties, such as mass, interaction range, or coupling strength.
  • Derive a specific observational prediction that differs from the predictions of established models.
  • Compare the prediction with published data and identify uncertainties and sources of error.
  • Accept, revise, or reject the hypothesis according to the results, not according to a desire to confirm it.

Conclusion

Water plays a central role in life, geology, and cosmic chemistry. Studying the origins of hydrogen and oxygen also helps explain the history of matter in the universe. The idea of an additional primordial constituent contributing to formation, growth, and stability, however, remains an exploratory hypothesis requiring a precise definition, specified equations, and reproducible evidence.

The most important next step is to turn the proposal into a measurable question: what new property would this constituent predict? How could it be observed in a way that could not be explained by gravity, chemistry, or established physics? Providing a quantitative answer is the route from speculation to scientific research.
